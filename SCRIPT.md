# "The Man Who Sold the Eiffel Tower" — original script v1 (2026-11-23)
Format: cartoon-3d-short (~60-68 s), ElevenLabs eleven_v4 voice (steady informative tone), no captions or labels.
Facts:
- In May 1925, Paris was still strained financially after WWI. The Eiffel Tower (built for the 1889 World's Fair, originally meant to stand 20 years) was rusting and expensive to maintain and repaint. A Paris newspaper article speculated the city might tear it down.
- Con artist "Count" Victor Lustig read the article in Paris and saw an opportunity. With accomplice Dan Collins ("Dapper Dan"), he forged stationery of the French Ministry of Posts and Telegraphs (Ministère des Postes et Télégraphes), posing as Deputy Director-General.
- Lustig summoned five prominent Parisian scrap-metal dealers to a strictly confidential meeting at the luxury Hôtel de Crillon, claiming the government was secretly selling the Eiffel Tower's ~7,000+ tons of iron for scrap to avoid public outcry.
- He hired a limousine to take the dealers to the tower for an inspection, watching for the most eager and insecure mark: André Poisson, an ambitious outsider desperate to enter Paris high society.
- Poisson's wife was suspicious of the secrecy and hotel venue. To overcome their doubt, Lustig arranged a private meeting and hinted that his low government salary required a bribe to guarantee Poisson's winning bid.
- Asking for a bribe convinced Poisson that Lustig was a genuine, corrupt French bureaucrat. Poisson handed over the funds and cash bribe (commonly cited around 70,000 francs), and Lustig took a train to Vienna, Austria.
- When Poisson discovered the fraud, he was too humiliated—and afraid of admitting he bribed a government official—to go to the police.
- Monitoring French newspapers from Vienna and seeing no mention of the crime, Lustig returned to Paris months later and ran the exact same con on five more scrap dealers.
- This time, the targeted dealer became suspicious and contacted the police before closing, forcing Lustig to flee to the United States.
- Lustig was later arrested by the U.S. Secret Service in 1935 for massive counterfeiting ("Lustig money"), sent to Alcatraz, and died of pneumonia in March 1947 at the federal medical center in Springfield, Missouri. His official death certificate listed his occupation as "apprentice salesman."
(Sources: Wikipedia "Victor Lustig"; Smithsonian Magazine, "The Man Who Sold the Eiffel Tower. Twice.", Mar 2016; Johnson / Sandford historical accounts; Paris Secret / Bonjour Paris)

In 1925, a con man sold the Eiffel Tower for scrap, without owning it.
Post-war Paris is broke, and its rusty landmark costs a fortune to repaint.
Reading the morning paper at a café, Victor Lustig hatches a plan.
He forges ministry letters and summons five scrap dealers to a secret luxury hotel.
Posing as a deputy minister, he says France is quietly scrapping seven thousand tons of iron.
He even takes them by limousine right under the tower.
Eager dealer André Poisson wants the fame, though his wife smells a rat.
So Lustig whispers that a small bribe will guarantee his bid.
Weirdly, that seals it: only a real official would ask for a bribe.
Poisson pays seventy thousand francs, and Lustig catches the next train to Vienna.
Luckily, Poisson gets a fancy receipt. Unluckily, France never agreed to sell it.
You see, a clean deal feels suspicious, while a shady shortcut traps the buyer in silence.
It's like buying a stolen watch in an alley: once it breaks, you can't call the cops.
Too ashamed to speak up, Poisson stays quiet. Seeing nothing in the papers, Lustig rides back to sell it again.
This time, the buyer calls the police, and Lustig bolts for America.
Federal agents finally caught him for counterfeiting, and his death certificate listed his job as apprentice salesman.
